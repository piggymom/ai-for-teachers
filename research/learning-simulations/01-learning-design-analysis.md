# Learning Design Analysis: MCC Decision Lab

The creator's central claim, paraphrased from the brief: traditional summative assessment gives teachers a snapshot of *what a student knew at the end*. A choice-based simulation captured turn by turn, with reflection prompts and timing data, gives teachers an *echocardiogram* of learning, an unfolding signal of how a student is actually thinking, deciding, and revising.

That is a real and serious idea. It deserves a real and serious evaluation.

## What the system actually captures

Walking the live tool and reading the API endpoints in the bundle (`/api/game/start`, `/api/game/make-choice`, `/api/game/submit-reflection`, `/api/admin/sessions`, `/api/admin/stats`), the per-session data captured is:

1. **Every choice made**, with the option text, the cost in resource, and the resulting stat deltas
2. **Every event the system rolled** for that student (because events are LLM-generated server-side, every student gets a different turn-by-turn sequence)
3. **Every reflection submitted**, free text plus which sentence-starter scaffold the student selected
4. **Skips**: when a student declined to reflect rather than reflect briefly
5. **Final stats**, time of game completion, success or failure title, and grade letter

Latent in that data, addressable through the instructor's "ask in plain English" panel and the cohort-level dashboards described in the bundle source:

- **Choice patterns aggregated as risk profile** ("bold / cautious / passive" tendency)
- **Stakeholder favoring** (which meters this student protects, which they sacrifice)
- **Painful drops hot-spots** (turns where many students lose stat heavily — discussion fodder)
- **Reflection theme search** (recurring concepts across submissions)
- **Reflection depth** (length, sentence-starter usage, skip rate)
- **Per-student turn-by-turn replay** (a recorded play of one student's session)

That is real, structured, behavioral data. It is the kind of data most ed-tech only fakes.

## Where the "echocardiogram" metaphor holds

The metaphor's strongest use is in three specific places:

**Decision-style fingerprinting under repeated pressure.** Across 16 turns and 3 forced reflection breaks, a student reveals a stable signal about how they handle competing claims (revenue vs. employee morale, public safety vs. community trust, accuracy vs. deadlines). That is genuine signal about a real cognitive disposition, not a knowledge inventory. A teacher reading "this student protects faculty morale at any cost, even when budget collapses" is reading something a final exam cannot show.

**The reflection corpus is content that didn't previously exist.** Asking 30 students to write a paragraph mid-game about "what assumptions are guiding your decisions?" produces 30 paragraphs of in-the-moment metacognition. Most courses generate zero such paragraphs in a semester. The corpus is searchable and aggregatable. Teachable themes ("most of my students wrote about being scared of risk" / "no one mentioned community impact") become legible at cohort scale.

**The instructor surface flips the polarity of formative assessment.** Instead of the teacher building a quiz, the system generates teachable moments out of student behavior and hands the teacher prepared discussion seeds: hot-spot turns, common reflection themes, choice-pattern outliers. This is a meaningful inversion. The teacher's prep work moves from "construct an instrument" to "interpret the data the students generated by playing."

If the claim is "this captures behavioral signal that summative assessment cannot, and turns that signal into teachable artifacts," I think the system delivers on that. With caveats.

## Where the metaphor breaks

An echocardiogram measures a continuous, involuntary process. This system measures a sequence of discrete chosen acts inside a stylized constraint. That is a different shape of data, and pretending otherwise will produce wrong inferences.

### What this system does not measure

**1. Conceptual understanding of the domain.** The "key terms" surfaced in turn narratives (Patent, Licensing Agreement, recidivism, accountability, emergency fund) are not gated. A student can pick the option that mentions a term they have never understood. There is no comprehension check. So the system cannot distinguish "this student understands what a licensing agreement is and chose against it for principled reasons" from "this student picked B because it was cheap." The reflection prompt might surface that distinction, but only if the student volunteers it.

**2. Skill execution.** The work of accounting is calculation, documentation, communication. The work of paralegal practice is research, citation, drafting. The simulation captures decisions about what to do, never the doing. A student who picks "thoroughly review the case file" learns nothing about how to review one. This is not necessarily a flaw, but it limits the assessment claim. This measures judgment about practice, not practice.

**3. Transfer.** Whether a student who handles the simulated trade-offs well will handle real ones is an empirical question with very thin evidence in the literature. Decision-making in stylized stakes (animated meters, fictional Lowell businesses) does not reliably transfer to decision-making in lived stakes (rent due, real client distress, regulatory exposure). The system would need transfer evidence to be defensible as a competence assessment.

**4. Misconception diagnosis.** This is the biggest gap. Because every choice produces a coherent narrative outcome with stat deltas, there is no way for the system to distinguish a *correct concept incorrectly applied* from a *missing concept entirely*. The student who thinks reputation damage is recoverable in three quarters and the student who has never heard the term reputation make the same kind of choices and get the same kind of outcomes. A real formative-assessment instrument would diagnose specifically what concept is missing or distorted. This system describes behavior; it does not diagnose understanding.

**5. The selection bias of turn events.** Because events are LLM-generated server-side at runtime, every student gets a different sequence. This is fine for replay variety. It is bad for assessment. Two students whose final stats differ may differ because they made different choices, OR because one of them got hit with the Audit Roulette in turn 3 and the other did not. The instructor cannot reliably control for the event variance. This makes it nearly impossible to use the system summatively, and it weakens cross-student comparison even formatively.

**6. Time-pressure as signal is contaminated.** Time-on-question is in the data, but it conflates: thinking carefully, reading slowly, walking away from the laptop, asking ChatGPT in another tab. Without proctoring or activity tracking, dwell-time is not a clean cognitive signal.

### Where the meter mechanic actively distorts

The deeper concern is that the meter system itself trains a specific epistemology of professional judgment that is probably wrong, or at least incomplete.

The system says: every decision moves four legible scoreboards. Your job is to keep all four above zero. You have a finite resource. Manage trade-offs across the dials.

That maps onto something real (most professional contexts do involve competing concerns), but it badly under-represents:

- **Timing.** When a problem becomes a problem matters. A real community justice director's hardest move is often deciding which problem to *not solve right now*. The meter system has no concept of "this issue should not be addressed yet."
- **Coalition and relationship.** Most consequential professional decisions are mediated by who you have on your side. A six-meter dashboard cannot represent a relationship with a stakeholder. The system only represents stakeholder *aggregates* (Community Trust at 47).
- **Ambiguity.** Real decisions are made under uncertainty about which dial they affect. The system always shows the resolution after the choice. So students never practice deciding under not-knowing.
- **The non-quantifiable.** A teacher's hardest professional decisions involve a child's wellbeing, a family's grief, the difference between truth and tact. Reducing those to a stat called "Student Success" is not just simplification, it is a model of practice that may train the wrong instincts.

Said most directly: this system trains *meter management*, then we infer that someone good at meter management will be good at the profession. That inference is unjustified. It is also the same inference the standardized-test era already taught us to distrust.

This is not a fatal critique. Meter management is one cognitive habit among several that matter. A simulation that practices it cleanly is fine. The problem is the *scope of the assessment claim*. Saying "this is an echocardiogram of learning" overclaims; saying "this is a structured way to surface decision-style and reflection-quality patterns across a cohort" undercitations its own value but is true.

## What this is genuinely useful for

Three uses I would defend without hedging.

**1. Pre/post comparison of decision style on the same scenario.** Run the same student through the same discipline at the start of a unit and again at the end. Do not compare across students (the LLM event variance makes this fragile), do compare within-student. The change in choice patterns and reflection content is a real measure of how their thinking moved.

**2. Cohort-level discussion seed.** The "painful drops hot-spots" and "common reflection themes" features turn the cohort's collective behavior into a 60-minute seminar plan that wrote itself. This is the use the instructor dashboard is best designed for and probably the best argument for the entire system.

**3. Surfacing affective and dispositional patterns that no test reaches.** "This student wrote about being afraid of risk in every reflection" is information a teacher cannot get from any other instrument. Whether that's actionable depends on context, but it is at minimum noticing-worthy.

## What this is not useful for

**Not useful for grading.** The event variance, the lack of misconception diagnosis, and the meter-distortion all argue against using this for any consequential assessment. A teacher who tries to grade students on their final stats will be grading partly on which random events the LLM rolled.

**Not useful as a stand-in for performance assessment in the real domain.** A nursing program that uses this in place of clinical hours is not training nurses. The simulation is decision-judgment practice, not skill practice.

**Not useful without instructor reading the reflections.** The whole formative-assessment claim depends on the teacher actually reading a stack of mid-game paragraphs and acting on what they see. If the dashboard becomes a place teachers glance at once a week, the system becomes ed-tech theater.

## What's missing for it to deliver on the claim

If I were designing the next version with the formative-assessment claim as the binding constraint:

- **Concept gating, not just key-term highlighting.** Some choices should be locked behind a brief concept check. Not punitive, just diagnostic. "Before you choose, can you tell us in one sentence what a licensing agreement is?" That single addition would convert the assessment claim from "behavioral signal" to "behavioral signal plus comprehension diagnosis," which is materially stronger.
- **Pre-determined event sequences for assessment runs.** Allow the instructor to switch the LLM events off and use a fixed scripted sequence when the goal is formative assessment, so cross-student comparison is valid.
- **A confidence rating with each choice.** "How sure are you?" Captures calibration, which is gold for formative assessment and trivially cheap to add.
- **Counterfactual replay.** After a decision, optionally reveal "here's what would have happened if you had chosen B." Builds the reasoning the system currently leaves implicit.
- **Reflection rubric the LLM applies.** Right now reflections are stored and searchable but unevaluated. A simple rubric pass (depth, perspective-taking, naming a tension, identifying assumption) would make the corpus actionable at scale.
- **A teacher-facing claim of what each session does and does not measure.** Currently the system implies it measures professional judgment. It would be more honest to surface, in the dashboard, "this session captured your students' decision style under stylized constraints and their written reflection on three prompts. It did not assess concept understanding, technical skill, or transfer."

## The portable lessons for AI literacy work

For the AI for Teachers project specifically, three things in this system are worth porting and one is worth avoiding.

**Port the reflection-as-mechanic pattern.** Mid-stream prompts, sentence-starter scaffolds, and a small mechanical reward for reflecting. This is the strongest single piece of design in the entire system. It works because it interrupts at the moment metacognition matters (during the decision, not after) and because it makes the reflection the teacher's source-of-truth artifact.

**Port the instructor-as-interpreter inversion.** The teacher's role is not to construct an assessment instrument; it is to read the artifacts students generate by playing. For AI literacy specifically, that could be teachers playing through scenarios about parent emails, AI-detection ambiguity, classroom integration tradeoffs, with the cohort's choices and reflections becoming the next session's discussion content.

**Port the textured failure narratives.** "The anxiety got so loud you stopped opening mail" is far more pedagogically useful than "you lost." Failure as story rather than score is portable to almost any learning context.

**Do not port the universal meter system.** AI literacy specifically is a domain where the cognitive demand is judgment under uncertainty about emerging tools, not balance across legible scoreboards. Building a four-meter "AI integration sim" would inherit the same epistemological distortion.
