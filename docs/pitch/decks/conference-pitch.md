## Slide 3: The Ozempic Analogy

**Ozempic works. Until you stop taking it.**

- Patients lose weight while on the drug
- When they stop, the weight comes back — because the underlying behaviors never changed
- The drug suppresses the struggle that builds lasting change

**Now look at AI in education:**

- MagicSchool generates lesson plans for teachers
- Khanmigo tutors students directly
- ChatGPT writes feedback, rubrics, and emails

**These tools work. Until you stop paying for them.**

## Slide 4: The Evidence

> Students using AI tutoring tools showed a **17% performance drop** when access was removed.

The tools worked *while they were on*. But no lasting capability was built.

**The question for every AI-in-education tool:**

What happens when the subscription lapses?
What happens when the district switches vendors?
What happens when the next model makes your interface obsolete?

## Slide 5: The Capacity Question

**Two models of AI in education:**

**Dependency Model**
- AI does the work → Teacher reviews → Output delivered
- Skill required: Clicking buttons
- When tool is removed: Capability disappears

**Capacity Model**
- Teacher learns the skill → Practices with any AI → Capability persists
- Skill required: Prompting, evaluation, iteration
- When tool is removed: Teacher keeps the skill

**Every AI tool you evaluate should be measured by this question:**
*Does the teacher get better, or just faster?*

## Slide 6: What Capacity Looks Like

"I don't replace teacher judgment. I develop it."

**The skill stack teachers actually need:**
1. Understanding what AI is (and isn't) — mental models, not magic
2. Structured prompting — not "be specific" but a transferable framework (Context, Constraints, Command, Criteria)
3. Evaluation — knowing when AI output is good enough and when it's dangerous
4. Iteration — improving AI output through targeted refinement
5. Integration — deciding where AI belongs in your practice and where it doesn't

**These skills transfer to any tool.** ChatGPT today, Gemini tomorrow, whatever comes next.

## Slide 7: The Learning Science

**SOLO Taxonomy** (Biggs & Collis, 1982) — 40 years of validation in education research.

Five levels of understanding:
1. **Pre-structural** — "I don't know what AI can do"
2. **Unistructural** — "I know I should be specific"
3. **Multistructural** — "I know the components of a good prompt"
4. **Relational** — "I understand *why* and *when* to use each component"
5. **Extended abstract** — "I can teach others and evaluate new AI tools critically"

**What if we could diagnose a teacher's level in real time and adapt instruction automatically?**

## Slide 9: What Adaptive Scaffolding Looks Like

**Same topic. Different teacher. Different instruction.**

**Pre-structural teacher asks about prompting:**
> Skippy: "Think of it like giving directions to someone who's never been to your school. You wouldn't just say 'come to the science room' — you'd say which building, which floor, turn left at the office. AI needs the same specificity. Let's try it with something you're working on this week."

**Relational teacher asks about prompting:**
> Skippy: "You've got the 4C framework down. But here's an edge case — when you're writing a differentiation prompt, which component is actually doing the heavy lifting? Is it Context or Constraints? And does that change depending on whether you're differentiating for access or rigor?"

**Same tutor. Different response. Based on real-time diagnostic data.**

## Slide 10: The External Testing Loop

**This is the most counterintuitive design decision we made.**

Most EdTech products: *Keep the user inside. Maximize engagement. Reduce churn.*

Our design: *"Go try this in ChatGPT. Come back and tell me what happened."*

**Why?**
- Transfer only happens when teachers practice in the tools they'll actually use
- Independence is the goal, not engagement metrics
- Districts trust a program that builds capability, not dependency
- When our subscription ends, the skills remain

"Teaching transferable skills is the ethical choice and the defensible moat."

## Slide 11: The Three Frameworks

**How do the major AI literacy frameworks compare?**

| | DOL Framework (Feb 2026) | OECD Framework (2025) | CDT Agency Framework (Harvard) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technical literacy | Strong | Strong | Included |
| Market literacy | Absent | Partial | Strong |
| Self-literacy | Absent | Absent | Strong |

The federal government tells teachers *how to use AI*.
The OECD adds *how AI works*.
Harvard's CDT asks: *Do you know your own values well enough to notice when AI shifts them?*

**We need all three.**

## Slide 12: The Self-Literacy Question

> "I think the question isn't whether you can use AI. It's whether you can use AI and still be recognizably yourself at the end of it."

**Week 6 of our curriculum — The Dependency Question:**

"If AI helps students produce better work, but they can't produce that work without AI, have they learned? How do you navigate that?"

We ask teachers to write a personal AI policy — not a school policy, a personal one. What are *your* values? Where are *your* lines? What will *you* commit to?

**That's the hardest conversation in AI literacy. And we built a curriculum around it.**

## Slide 14: What We've Learned

**Building AI tools for teachers, not just with AI:**

1. **Teachers don't need more content.** They need skills and confidence.
2. **Personalization isn't optional.** A 3rd grade reading teacher and an AP Physics teacher need fundamentally different AI coaching.
3. **Artifacts beat conversations.** If they don't walk away with something they'll use Monday, it didn't work.
4. **Independence is the metric.** If they can't do it without you, you haven't taught them anything.
5. **The ethical choice is the strategic one.** Building capacity instead of dependency is both right and defensible.
