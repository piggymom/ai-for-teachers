# Problem Statement

## The AI Literacy Crisis in K-12 Education

Artificial intelligence is transforming every sector of the economy, yet the 3.7 million public school teachers in the United States — the people responsible for preparing the next generation — are receiving almost no meaningful training on how to understand, use, or teach about AI.

**The gap is urgent and widening.** A 2024 RAND Corporation survey found that while 40% of teachers report using AI tools in their practice, fewer than 25% have received any formal training. Most are self-teaching through trial and error, developing habits and misconceptions that may be difficult to unlearn. Meanwhile, students are using AI tools daily, often without guidance, creating a knowledge inversion where students understand the tools better than their teachers.

**Current solutions are failing.** The dominant approach to teacher AI professional development — one-off workshops, vendor-led webinars, and self-paced video courses — suffers from three fundamental problems:

1. **Generic, not personalized.** A 3rd-grade reading teacher and a high school chemistry teacher have radically different AI needs, yet they sit in the same workshop. Current PD treats AI literacy as a single skill rather than a contextual practice.

2. **Passive, not constructive.** Watching someone demonstrate ChatGPT is not the same as building a prompt for your own lesson plan. Most PD focuses on tool demonstrations rather than skill construction. Teachers leave with awareness but not capability.

3. **Dependency, not independence.** Many AI PD programs are thinly disguised product trainings — teaching teachers to use one specific tool rather than developing transferable skills. When the tool changes (and AI tools change monthly), the training becomes obsolete.

**The equity dimension is critical.** Teachers in under-resourced schools — disproportionately serving students of color and low-income communities — are least likely to receive quality AI professional development. Without intervention, AI literacy becomes another axis of educational inequality: well-resourced schools integrate AI thoughtfully while others either avoid it entirely or adopt it without critical understanding.

**The cost of inaction is measured in student outcomes.** Teachers who lack AI literacy cannot model responsible AI use, cannot design AI-enhanced learning experiences, cannot help students develop critical AI thinking skills, and cannot make informed decisions about when AI helps and when it harms. Every month of delay compounds the gap between students who learn to think critically about AI and those who don't.

The problem is not that teachers resist AI — surveys consistently show teacher curiosity outpaces their confidence. The problem is that no one has built a professional development experience that respects their intelligence, fits their schedule, connects to their classroom reality, and builds lasting capability rather than temporary enthusiasm.
