# Grant Writing Guide — Best Practices

## Voice and Tone

### Do:
- Write with **confident clarity**. You're solving a real problem with a proven approach.
- Use **active voice**: "Skippy diagnoses each teacher's level" not "each teacher's level is diagnosed."
- Be **specific over vague**: "$8-15 per teacher" beats "cost-effective." "70% completion rate target" beats "high engagement."
- Show **respect for the funder's intelligence** — don't over-explain basics. If they fund education, they know what PD is.
- **Lead with impact**, not technology. Funders care about outcomes for teachers and students. The AI architecture is the "how," not the "what."

### Don't:
- Don't use jargon without definition. "SOLO taxonomy" needs a parenthetical the first time.
- Don't oversell. "Revolutionary" and "groundbreaking" trigger skepticism. Let the evidence speak.
- Don't be vague about numbers. If you don't have data yet, say "target" or "projected" — don't leave it out.
- Don't copy-paste the same application to every funder. Each funder has specific priorities — reflect them.
- Don't bury the ask. State clearly what you want, how much, and for what duration.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Solution looking for a problem
Don't start with "We built an AI tutor" — start with "Teachers lack AI literacy and current PD doesn't work." The problem should make the solution feel inevitable.

### 2. Feature lists instead of outcomes
Funders don't care that you have "a 6-layer prompt composition system." They care that "teachers advance at least one SOLO level in 7 weeks." Lead with outcomes, support with how.

### 3. Unrealistic budgets
A $50K grant with $45K for personnel and $5K for everything else looks like a salary request, not a project budget. Include meaningful costs for evaluation, operations, and dissemination.

### 4. No sustainability plan
"We'll seek additional grant funding" is not a sustainability plan. Show a path to earned revenue or self-sufficiency. Funders want to invest in something that outlasts their grant.

### 5. Ignoring the evaluation
Many proposals treat evaluation as an afterthought. For education grants, the evaluation plan is often the most scrutinized section. Budget for it. Design it. Name who will do it.

### 6. Mission drift to match the funder
If a funder prioritizes school choice and your project is about teacher PD, don't contort your project to fit. Apply to funders whose priorities naturally align.

## How to Quantify Impact

### Use the "Compared to What" Framework
Every impact claim should answer: compared to what?

**Weak:** "AI for Teachers improves teacher AI literacy."
**Strong:** "In our pilot, teachers advanced an average of 1.5 SOLO levels over 7 weeks — comparable to gains typically seen in semester-long university courses, delivered in 2 hours total."

**Weak:** "The program is cost-effective."
**Strong:** "At $8-15 per teacher, AI for Teachers costs 50-100x less than a traditional multi-day PD workshop ($500-2,000) while delivering personalized instruction that workshops cannot."

**Weak:** "Teachers found it valuable."
**Strong:** "NPS score of 62, with 85% of completers reporting they used at least one artifact in their classroom within 30 days."

### Before You Have Data (Pre-Pilot)
Use "projected" and "target" language with clear basis:
- "Based on intelligent tutoring system meta-analyses (VanLehn, 2011), we project learning gains of d = 0.5-0.7."
- "Our target completion rate is 70%, based on the 20-minute session design that fits within a single prep period."
- "We anticipate $8-15 per teacher in marginal delivery cost, based on current API pricing and our token-usage projections."

### After You Have Data (Post-Pilot)
Lead with results. Use confidence intervals where possible:
- "Teachers advanced an average of 1.5 SOLO levels (95% CI: 1.2-1.8)."
- "Course completion rate was 78% (n=50), compared to the 30-50% typical of online PD."
- "Per-teacher delivery cost was $11.40, confirming the sub-$15 projection."

## Storytelling in Grant Applications

### The Story Arc
Every strong grant application tells a story with four beats:

1. **The world as it is** (problem): Teachers are navigating AI without training. Current PD fails them.
2. **What could be different** (vision): Every teacher could have a personalized AI literacy coach.
3. **How we make it happen** (solution): Skippy — 7 weeks, 7 artifacts, 2 hours total.
4. **What we need from you** (ask): Funding to prove it works and scale it.

### Use a Real Teacher
When possible, include a brief vignette (real or composite):

> "Maria teaches 7th-grade science in the Bronx. She's heard about AI from her students, who use ChatGPT for homework. She tried it once herself but didn't know what to ask and got a generic response. She closed the tab and hasn't opened it since. Maria doesn't need a workshop — she needs a conversation. In Week 2 of AI for Teachers, she built a prompt template for generating lab report feedback that she now uses every Friday. In Week 4, she refined it into a rubric calibration tool. She's not an AI expert — but she's an AI-literate teacher who can make informed decisions about when and how to use these tools with her students."

### Numbers + Stories = Persuasion
Neither numbers alone nor stories alone are sufficient. Use stories to make numbers feel real:
- "78% of pilot teachers completed all 7 weeks — teachers like Maria, who squeezed 20-minute sessions into her prep period between 2nd and 3rd block."

## Responding to Reviewer Concerns

### "Is this just a chatbot wrapper?"
**Response:** No. Skippy is a diagnostic-adaptive tutoring system with a 6-layer prompt composition engine, an asynchronous conversation classifier running SOLO-based diagnostics, a state machine tracking learning phases and skill components, and a dual-strategy artifact extraction system. A chatbot answers questions. Skippy guides a structured learning progression, diagnoses understanding in real time, and adapts scaffolding accordingly.

### "Will this become obsolete as AI tools change?"
**Response:** AI for Teachers teaches transferable skills — mental models, prompting frameworks, critical evaluation, ethical reasoning — not tool-specific tricks. The 4C Framework (Context, Constraints, Command, Criteria) works with any AI tool, current or future. We explicitly send teachers to test their skills in multiple AI tools to ensure transfer.

### "How is this different from just using ChatGPT for PD?"
**Response:** ChatGPT is a tool; Skippy is a tutor. ChatGPT will answer any question but can't structure a learning progression, diagnose misconceptions, assess readiness levels, or ensure you're building transferable skills. Skippy does all of these things through 4,134 lines of pedagogically-grounded prompt content, SOLO taxonomy integration, and real-time conversation classification.

### "What if teachers don't complete the 7 weeks?"
**Response:** We've designed for this. Sessions are 20 minutes (fits in a prep period). Each session produces a usable artifact (immediate value). The course uses "One Win, Then Wrap" — teachers leave every session with something they can use, even if they don't complete the full 7 weeks. Our target is 70%+ completion, which we monitor in real time and can support with nudge communications.

### "Is this scalable, or does it require per-teacher customization?"
**Response:** The personalization is automated. The AI handles all adaptation — adjusting prompts based on teacher profile, diagnosing SOLO level, adapting scaffolding, extracting artifacts. The marginal cost per additional teacher is $8-15 in API costs. No additional facilitators, no additional content creation. This is what makes universal teacher AI literacy economically feasible.
